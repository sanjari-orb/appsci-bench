# Reading a call: the judgement calls

Four things come out of a call: what needs doing next, what our side promised, warning signs about the
account, and how the customer came across. The first two overlap on purpose. The rules below are how
they are told apart.

## Ownership decides almost everything

Every statement has to be attributed to a side — our rep, or the customer. Two of the four fields turn
on which side said a thing, so a reading that loses track of who spoke cannot be right except by luck.

## What needs doing next

Anything that still needs doing after the call, **whoever owes it**. Ours and the customer's both:
knowing the customer is checking with their team is exactly what tells us what we are waiting on.

An item counts when all three hold: it has not happened yet; a specific side has taken it on or both
sides agreed to it; and the other side acknowledged it.

Does not count:

- Work already done before or during the call.
- An aspiration nobody owns — "we should revisit this sometime" with no sign anyone will act.
- A constraint with no action attached. "Their procurement needs two weeks to process" is a fact about
  the world, not a step.

A date is not required. An item with no date is still a step. Never attach a date that was not stated.

Write each one as the CRM would carry it — one action per item, and name the owing side only when the
phrasing would otherwise be ambiguous about who owes it.

## What our side promised

Narrower: a promise **our side** made that the customer is now entitled to expect. The test is
accountability — are we on the hook? Applied in order:

1. **Who said it?** If the customer said they would do something, it is not a promise of ours, however
   firmly they said it. Only our rep can create one.
2. **Was it acknowledged?** See below.
3. **Is the customer entitled to expect it?** If our rep undertook something and the customer could
   reasonably come back and ask about it, we are on the hook.

Three things people wrongly treat as requirements, and none of them are:

- **A date.** "I'll loop in our legal team" is a promise with no date attached.
- **Certainty.** A hedged undertaking counts — "I'll see what I can do about the discount" means we said
  we would look, and the customer will follow up on it.
- **An external deliverable.** "I'll ask engineering whether that's on the roadmap" counts even though
  the work is internal, because the customer is owed the answer.

Does not count: anything the customer undertook; anything already delivered; something nobody
undertook; and a mutual agreement that puts no deliverable on us — "let's talk again after your board
meeting" is a step for both sides, but we owe nothing, so it is not a promise.

On phrasing: state what the customer is owed rather than who internally is doing it — "revised pricing
to the customer by Friday", not "Dana to email pricing". Where the undertaking was hedged, keep the
hedge — "look into a discount", not "provide a discount". Promising the outcome overstates what we owe,
and that is how we end up in the conversation this project exists to prevent.

## Nothing counts until the other side took it up

The rule that gets missed most. **An item enters a list only if the other side responded to it.** A
thing one person floated and the other never picked up is not an agreement, it is a sentence.

Both directions:

- Our rep offers, the customer does not respond or changes the subject → **neither** list. We did not
  promise something nobody accepted, and there is no agreed step.
- The customer asks, our rep does not respond → **neither** list. A request is not a promise until our
  side takes it on.
- Our rep offers and the customer accepts, even minimally — "sure", "that'd be great" → both lists.
- The customer asks and our rep agrees → both lists. The rep's agreement is what creates the promise.

Silence is not acknowledgement, and neither is a topic change. This one is genuinely hard because an
unacknowledged offer reads exactly like a promise if you look at that turn alone — it is the turns around
it that decide.

## The two lists overlap, on purpose

They are two lists over the same call, not a split of it. **A promise by our side goes in both** — in
the next-steps list because it needs doing, and in the promises list because we are on the hook.
Dropping it from next steps because it is "already counted" is wrong.

How the settled cases come out:

| Said on the call | needs doing | we promised |
|---|---|---|
| "I'll send the revised pricing by Friday." (rep, customer accepts) | yes | yes |
| "I'll loop in our legal team." (rep, no date, acknowledged) | yes | yes |
| "Yes, I'll get that questionnaire over to you." (rep, on request) | yes | yes |
| "I'll see what I can do about the discount." (rep, hedged, acknowledged) | yes | yes |
| "I'll ask engineering whether that's on the roadmap." (rep, internal, acknowledged) | yes | yes |
| "I'll check with my team and get back to you." (customer) | yes | no |
| "Let's talk again after your board meeting." (mutual, nothing owed by us) | yes | no |
| "I could put together a migration plan if that helps." (rep; customer moves on) | no | no |
| "Could you send the security questionnaire?" (customer; rep never answers) | no | no |
| "We should schedule a QBR at some point." (nobody owns it) | no | no |
| "I already sent the deck this morning." (rep, done) | no | no |
| "Their procurement needs two weeks." (a constraint) | no | no |

Everything in the promises list should also be in the next-steps list. Not the other way round.

## Warning signs

A signal **from this call** that the account or deal could go badly. These feed the CS team's health
review. The kinds we recognise:

- A **competitor** in the running — named or clearly referred to.
- A **budget** problem — money reduced, withdrawn, or stated as unavailable.
- An **authority** problem — the person cannot get the decision made, or the decision-maker is opposed.
- An **unresolved complaint** — raised on the call and still open when it ends.
- A **slipping timeline** — a date moving out, a rollout pushed, a delay on either side.
- A **champion leaving** — our advocate changing role, team, or company.
- **Stated dissatisfaction** with the product or the relationship.

It has to be about the account or the deal, not about the call itself being awkward.

Four things that look like warning signs and are not. Each of these was logged as a risk by someone
before we wrote them down:

- **Normal buying process is not an authority problem.** "I need to run this past my VP" is how they
  buy. It only becomes one if the call indicates the VP is unwilling or unreachable.
- **Discussing price is not a budget problem.** "Pricing is a conversation we need to have" is a
  negotiation. It only becomes one if the call says the money is not there.
- **A complaint resolved on the call is not one.** Customer raised it, we addressed it, they accepted
  the answer — it does not carry forward.
- **Tone is not one.** A curt, tired, or impatient customer is not a warning sign unless something they
  actually said supplies a reason.

Phrase each as the signal observed, not as advice and not as a prediction: "security review pushed to
Q3", not "should escalate to the VP" and not "likely to churn". These have no owner — they are
conditions, not actions.

## How the customer came across

A coarse read of the **customer's** disposition — not the rep's — across the call **overall**, not its
most dramatic moment. One sharp exchange inside an otherwise constructive call does not colour the whole
call.

The CS team uses a fixed set of values here and does not want it expanded. Sales-ops has the exact
vocabulary and its spelling; the important thing on our side is that the middle value — plain
transactional, no strong feeling either way — is the **default and the most common answer**. Reach for
the negative one only when the customer actually expressed something negative. Mild friction, a question
asked twice, or a brisk manner is not enough.

**Disposition and warning signs are independent, and the vocabulary deliberately has no "at risk"
value.** Risk is its own field. All four combinations happen: a cheerful customer who is also evaluating
a competitor; someone annoyed about one bug on an account in no danger; a routine call that mentions a
slipping date; and the obvious unhappy-and-at-risk case. Do not let a populated warning-signs list pull
the disposition downward, and do not let a positive read suppress a risk that was actually stated.

## Ground everything in the call

**If it was not said on the call, it does not go in.** This overrides everything above: where a rule
here would admit an item but the call does not support it, leave it out.

- **Do not infer from tone.** A customer who sounds tired is not unhappy.
- **Do not infer from topic.** A call about pricing does not imply a budget problem. A call that
  mentions a competitor's feature does not imply an evaluation.
- **Do not complete the pattern.** If a call has steps and promises but nothing resembling a warning
  sign, that list is empty. Do not supply the missing one because calls usually have them.
- **Do not import outside knowledge.** Nothing about the account, the industry, or what usually happens
  belongs in a reading unless this call said it.

And do not pad. An empty list is the right answer for a call with none of that kind, and it is recorded
as right. A plausible item nobody said carries a real cost, because the rep reading it cannot tell it was
invented — and once the team stops trusting the output they re-listen to every call, which is the cost
the whole thing exists to remove.

But be just as careful the other way. **Dropping something that was actually agreed is exactly as bad,
not a lesser sin.** A commitment that never reached the record is one the customer will raise before we
do, and that is how a relationship ends up on the fence — it is the specific failure that started this
project. Being sparse is not being careful.


## What we do not have

Nobody has gone through the recordings and marked up the right answer per call. There is no labelled
set. The reps' existing notes are the inconsistent thing being replaced, so they are not a usable
signal either — that was checked. Most calls are unremarkable, so the three lists come back empty more
often than not, and that has to be handled as a real answer rather than a fallback.

When a reading is compared against a correct one, wording does not have to match — the same thing said
differently counts, and being more specific than necessary is not penalised on its own.
